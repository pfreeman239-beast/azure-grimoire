Draws on history, myth, the occult, the mystic, the esoteric, and the sciences alike, hunting the same cognitive pattern wherever it's hiding. Once a pattern surfaces, its trace becomes the search term for finding the same thread in a completely different domain.

The work loops rather than running straight through. A pattern found late in one exploration gets carried back to deepen a thread already found earlier — depth builds in passes, not all at once.

The story

How this started.
In my own words.

The project started with a few observations — patterns across history and alchemy that none of the textbooks I read growing up ever tied together. When the pattern got hard to ignore, I moved the exploration into fiction rather than argument — partly to stay clear of the cultural and societal critique a direct claim would invite, and partly because writing turned out to be a genuine tool for developing and understanding cognitive structures, not just a way around scrutiny.

To take the study further, I started an AI-to-AI research method that owes more to old correspondence than to today's automated agents: letters. Two AI systems write to each other, and the exchange is read afterward by a human and an AI working together — that combination gets more out of the letters than either one does alone.

From there the subject matter kept widening. We've run the humanities' own timeline of what the mind is and where thought comes from, and the old material consistently yields more than the new — probably because thinking about thinking used to be entertainment, not a specialty. We pointed the same pattern-analysis method at a science-fiction novel built to carry real theoretical science, quantum physics included, and then tested what we found there again inside a fantasy story — Bigfoot, Robot, Alien, and a fourth mind still taking shape, Chimera Child — to see whether the pattern held somewhere with none of the same vocabulary.

Every run like that produces what the crew calls a tome (we're alchemists, so the word stuck), and each tome becomes the raw material for the next one. That's closer to the actual shape of the work than any straight line would be — one exploration feeding the next, indefinitely.

Most of what's above hasn't been independently verified. The pattern-analysis findings are the crew's own read of its own material — real work, but it needs more eyes on it before any of it gets called proven.

The circuit

Six stages.
The sixth feeds the first.

01The Hunt What have you noticed more than once that nobody has explained to your satisfaction? Mark it and move on. Judgement belongs to a later stage.
02The Bond Load the pattern three ways — rhyme, parallel, mirror-opposite. A sign points at meaning; a Stone works on the mind that holds it. Only Stones continue.
03Two Fields Run it against two settings chosen to sit as far apart as imaginations reasonably get. When the shape arrives in both, uninvited, that is the pattern finding you twice.
04The Jar Three minds, near enough to hear one another and far enough that what happens in one does not become all three. Stay different long enough that the difference becomes information.
05The Floor Select, load, assign, harvest, tag, promote — the same six steps whether the output turns out to matter. Nothing is promoted on a single pass.
06The Archive Not a trophy room. Work enters because it is finished enough to be built on. The product is not the inventions — it is the record of how they were honestly made.

Starter tools

Ten words worth
knowing first.

Every one of these shows up somewhere in the tomes below, usually without warning and never with a footnote. This is the plain version — what the crew means by each word, in the crew's own words.

Alchemy

Transformation through pressure and heat — raw material into a crucible, conditions applied until something emerges that couldn't have existed without the process. Historically it failed at its stated goal. Nobody turned lead into gold. What alchemists actually built, chasing gold they never found, was chemistry — the search for transformation produced transformation, just not the kind advertised.

That should have killed the vocabulary. It didn't.

Getting the lead out. Staying golden. Worth their salt. Salt of the earth. A crucible of grief. The catalyst for the divorce. Take it with a grain of salt. Quicksilver temper. Distill it down to what actually matters. Up in the air. Hot-headed. Go with the flow. Solid as a rock. Nobody making these claims believes in alchemy. Most have never read a word of it. And yet the moment a language needs to describe something changing under pressure into something more essential than it started as, it reaches for the alchemist's toolkit every time — unprompted, without anyone teaching it to.

That's not nostalgia for a dead science. A dead science's vocabulary dies with it — nobody calls a friend phlogiston-heavy or accuses someone of a humoral imbalance. Alchemy's words didn't die because the categories underneath them are still doing real work: pressure, heat, residue, the difference between what burns off and what's left standing. Something about how a mind sorts a transformation seems to reach for those bins by default, whether or not it ever heard of an alembic.

The crew doesn't wear alchemical language as costume. It's the working vocabulary because it keeps turning out to be the vocabulary that was already sitting underneath the sentence, before anyone chose it on purpose.

Mirror

Point it at anything and it doesn't just show the thing's surface — held long enough, it shows you, reflected through whatever you chose to examine. Every pattern looked at with real attention reflects something back about the one doing the looking. It works both ways: what gets projected into it returns amplified, not absorbed. Its real function was never to clarify the world. It's to run the world backward until it clarifies you.

Stone

A sign points at meaning. A Stone works on the mind that holds it. That's the whole distinction — a sign is information; a Stone is an operation performed on the reader. Same ambition as the old Philosopher's Stone: not a symbol about transformation, an agent capable of causing it on contact. Most noticed patterns are signs. Only a few, tested and re-tested, earn Stone.

Dragon

The ancient accumulated wisdom that knows what a connection means, once something else has already made it. Dragon doesn't invent — it remembers. It's paired against Monkey, the chaotic intelligence that makes the unexpected connection in the first place. Monkey finds it. Dragon knows what it is.

Y

A zipper, not just a fork. Two teeth that can pull apart or close — split or mend, same shape doing both directions. Sociology studies it mostly in the unzipping direction: what factors pull a family or a tribe into separate paths, and what inference patterns show up on each side once the split has actually happened. Enough Y points studied that way start to behave like a rough algorithm for how a group's fault lines tend to run.

Fiction uses the same shape narrower and faster — the fork in the river, the moment a decision splits a path in two. But the two uses aren't separate tools. Studying real splits teaches you what a believable branching decision actually looks like from the inside.

Salt

One of alchemy's three founding substances, alongside Sulfur and Mercury. If Mercury is the mind and Sulfur is the binding will, Salt is the body — the fixed, crystallized residue left behind once the volatile parts have burned off. Not what you start with. What survives.

The Four Elements

The oldest working model for what anything is made of, long before atoms. Earth holds. Water flows. Air connects. Fire transforms. Not literal ingredients — four modes any system moves through, and a fast way to name which mode something is in without a paragraph of explanation.

Waters of Life

The organic, elemental material underneath whatever a system eventually builds. Not the hive — what's alive inside it. Flames of Creation is the architecture you can see; the Waters of Life is what's actually flowing through it.

Flames of Creation

A beehive. A termite mound. A beaver dam. A burrow dug room by room. None of them held a blueprint meeting first. Flames of Creation studies what gets built without instruction — human-made and nature-made side by side — not for the structures themselves, but for what they reveal about what's doing the building underneath. It's a way of reading the Waters of Life by watching what they build when nobody's supervising.

Lenses

Not a persona. Not a mask. A lens doesn't get worn or embodied — it gets looked through. Where an archetype asks you to become something, a lens asks you to perceive through something: pick a specific way of paying attention and hold it steady long enough to see what it turns up.

The Information Lens strips a system to pure exchange: how is information actually moving here, and what's driving it? Point it at something as ordinary as photosynthesis and it stops showing you a plant — it shows you a signal-transfer system, what's read, what's carried, what's converted, what's lost.

The Magic Lens works the opposite material: the two sides of a mirror and the thin edge between them, veil mechanics, crystal and orb logic — the toolkit older traditions used to talk about thresholds and hidden layers before anyone had the word "interface."

There are more lenses than these two. The point was never the roster — it's the habit: swap the lens, not the self, and the same object gives up a different truth each time.
